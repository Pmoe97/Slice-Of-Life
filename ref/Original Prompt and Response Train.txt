1. Identity

An apartment living sim. The player is one of five roommates in a four-bedroom apartment; everything else is common space. The player works from home on a computer for money and orders anything they need for delivery. The entire game takes place inside the apartment.

Target feel: drama, comedy, romance, slice-of-life RPG. Part visual novel, part small open world. The apartment is the world; depth comes from the people in it, not from map size. Stories unfold naturally rather than being dispensed. The player always has something meaningful to do — daily goals with real consequences, plus longer questlines and relationship arcs.

Tone and content settings live in a single CONTENT_CONFIG constant. The game is drama-first and mature themes are not gated by design; do not hardcode tone assumptions anywhere in the logic layer.

2. Platform constraints

Single monolithic Perchance generator. State the plugins you're using up front. All persistence via localStorage with a stateVersion integer and a migrate(state) function stubbed from day one — I will iterate on this for months and players will have live saves.

3. Architecture

Monolithic file, but strict internal boundaries. Section order, each delimited with <!-- ===== SECTION: name ===== --> and a matching close comment:

TOKENS — CSS custom properties only
STYLES — all CSS
MARKUP — semantic HTML shell + <template> elements
CONFIG — every tunable number, every content flag, character bibles
STATE — schema, defaults, save/load/migrate
SIM — pure functions: clock, schedules, off-screen resolution, needs
NPC — memory, relationships, mood, context assembly
LLM — prompt construction, call, response parsing, validation

RENDER — state → DOM, idempotent
UI — event delegation, input handling
BOOT — init sequence

No section reaches across a boundary. SIM never touches DOM. RENDER never mutates state. LLM never writes to state directly — it returns a proposal that NPC/SIM validate and apply.

4. Layout

Fixed five-region grid (see reference image), CSS Grid, full viewport height, no page scroll — regions scroll internally.

Header: day, date, in-game clock, money, current room, energy/needs bar, save/menu.
Left sidebar: navigation. Room list with occupancy indicators (who you know is in there). Player self-panel below.
Main content: the scene. Layered image at top, narration and dialogue log below it, scrolling.
Right sidebar: context. Who is present, their visible mood, active goals/quests, inventory, delivery/order access.
Footer: free-text input bar plus contextual action chips.

Responsive: below ~900px, sidebars collapse into toggleable drawers over the main content. Never stack into a long scroll.

6. Time and simulation

Clock advances in discrete blocks; actions cost time. Every tick, all NPCs resolve — not just present ones.

Off-screen resolution must be cheap and deterministic: zero LLM calls. NPCs follow schedule tables (weekday/weekend/state-modified) with weighted random events drawn from tables in CONFIG. Results are written as compact structured event records. Those records are narrated only when the player observes evidence, hears about them secondhand, or an NPC recalls them — narration is generated at the moment of contact, not at the moment of occurrence.

This is the feature that makes the apartment feel alive. Roommates must be able to fight, cook, break something, have a bad day at work, or bring someone home while the player is asleep in their room.

7. Cast generation

Roommates are generated per playthrough and frozen. No two houses are alike. The generator runs once at new-game, produces complete character bibles, writes them to state.npcs[id].bible, and that bible is thereafter immutable and injected verbatim into every context. It is never regenerated, never summarized, never rewritten by the LLM.

7.1 Trait pools

CONFIG defines weighted pools, each entry carrying tags and exclusions:

Core temperament — a small set of orthogonal axes with numeric values (warmth, volatility, openness, conscientiousness, assertiveness, self-awareness), not adjectives. Speech and behavior derive from these numbers.
Occupation — with schedule template, income band, stress profile, and hours attached.
Interests — 2–3 drawn from a tagged pool; tags drive compatibility scoring.
Values — 2 held strongly; values carry opposition pairs so the generator can create real friction.
Baggage — one formative history item that explains a behavior pattern.
Wound — the thing they're defensive about.
Want — the concrete thing they're pursuing during the game.
Blind spot — one belief about themselves that is false. Mandatory. No character ships without one.
Boundary — a line that matters to them, and what happens when it's crossed.
Speech profile — verbosity, formality, humor style, profanity level, verbal tics, texting style.
Visual descriptors — locked appearance string plus generation seed.

Constraints the generator must satisfy:

No two roommates share a core occupation category.
No two share more than one interest tag.
Temperament axes must span a range — reject casts where all four cluster.
At least one high-volatility and one low-volatility character.
Schedules must overlap and conflict; at least one roommate is home during hours others aren't.
7.2 Relational pass

After individuals are rolled, a second pass generates the cast web. For every pair:

Prior relationship (how long they've known each other, how they met, who came first)
Baseline relationship axes — asymmetric by default; A may trust B more than B trusts A
One shared history beat, positive or negative
Compatibility and friction scores derived from values-opposition and temperament distance

Then, cast-level requirements, all mandatory:

One active unresolved conflict between two roommates that predates the player and is visible in play
One alliance or closeness the player is outside of
One secret held by one roommate that at least one other suspects
At least one roommate whose want directly obstructs another's want

These are what the story grows out of. A cast that fails any of these is rejected and rerolled.

7.3 Generation modes

New-game offers:

Full random — everything rolled from a seed
Guided — player specifies constraints (count, archetype nudges, tone dial, relationship openness, content preferences), generator fills the rest
Manual — full authoring of one or more roommates through a structured form; mixed casts allowed
Seed entry — paste a seed and get an identical house

Everything is driven by state.seed so a seed reproduces a cast exactly. Surface the seed in the UI — players will want to share houses.

7.4 Generation method

Structured draws happen in JS, deterministically, from the seeded PRNG — not by asking the LLM to pick. The LLM's only job in generation is to write flavor prose for a draw that has already been made: name, physical description, one-paragraph history, sample speech lines. It receives the drawn structure and expands it. It never chooses traits, never chooses relationships, and never invents a trait not in the pools.

If an LLM generation call fails, fall back to templated prose from the structured draw. Character creation must never hard-fail.

7.5 Post-generation

Once written, the bible is read-only. Everything that changes about a character afterward lives in the mutable layers — mood, needs, relationship axes, memory, arcs, flags. Growth is expressed as deviation from the bible, not edits to it. If a character changes over the course of a playthrough, that change is legible precisely because the baseline is fixed.

8. NPC memory (revised)

Unchanged in structure — facts, decaying episodes, rolling summary, hard context budget — with two additions:

Context assembly injects the frozen bible plus the relevant slice of the cast web: what this character knows and feels about the other roommates, not just about the player. Ivy's read on Marcus is part of who Ivy is in a scene.
The pre-existing shared-history beats seed each NPC's episode log at day zero with backdated entries. The house has a past before the player's first turn.

9. Imagery

Layered composition: background (room × time-of-day × lighting) + character layer(s) + overlay. Backgrounds are generated once per room/time/state combination and cached in state by key — never regenerate an identical scene. Each character has a locked visual descriptor and a stable seed for consistency across scenes; expression and pose vary via prompt suffix.

Show placeholders immediately and swap in generated images asynchronously. The game must never block on an image.

7 — Household model (new section, replaces the fixed five-roommate assumption)

The apartment has four bedrooms, each with capacity 2. The player always occupies one. Residency is dynamic and ranges from 1 to 8 NPC residents — the player may choose to live with a single roommate or a full house, and this changes over the course of play.

Second beds are not filled by default. A room becomes shared only through an in-fiction reason: a couple, a partner moving in, an agreement, or financial pressure. A roommate may move a partner in. A roommate may eventually move into the player's room. Room assignment is mutable state, not config.

Every character carries a residency status: resident, partner_of_resident, visitor, prospective, former. Non-residents exist and matter — visitors, sleepovers, partners who aren't on the lease. Only residents contribute rent.

Rent is a live system. Total rent is fixed; the per-person share is a function of contributor count. Someone leaving raises everyone's share and creates pressure to fill the room. This is a primary driver of both economics and drama — treat it as a first-class loop, not flavor.

Move-in and move-out are gameplay the player participates in: interviewing prospects, vetoing or approving, asking someone to leave, being asked to accommodate a partner. NPCs also initiate departures on their own from arc state.

A departed character is archived, never deleted. Their bible, memory, and relationship history persist under residency: "former". They can return, be referenced, be missed, or show up unannounced. Remaining roommates retain all memory of them.

The character generator is therefore not a new-game-only routine. It is a service callable at any point in play to produce a new character, and the cast web must support incremental pair creation and removal.

§7.x — Cast constraints scale to household size (replaces the mandatory four-person requirements)

Previously stated cast-level requirements assumed four roommates. Constraints are now tiered by resident count:

1 resident: no cast-level requirements possible. All drama is dyadic, so the single character must carry it — require a strong active want, a sharp wound, and a live blind spot. Reject flat characters harder at this tier than at any other.
2–3 residents: require at least one friction pair and one asymmetric relationship.
4+ residents: full requirements as previously specified — unresolved conflict predating the player, an alliance the player is outside of, a held secret, obstructing wants.

When a character is added mid-game, evaluate constraints against the resulting household and bias the new character's draw toward whatever the house currently lacks. A house with no volatility should tend to roll someone volatile.

Constraint satisfaction must never loop indefinitely. Cap generation attempts, then relax constraints in a defined priority order and record which were dropped in CONFIG-visible debug output.

§8 — Central character schema (replaces trait-pool section 7.1 as the top-level authority)

There is one canonical character schema defined in CONFIG. It declares every field, its type, its default, whether it is required, and its valid range or pool. Nothing anywhere in the codebase constructs a character object by hand.

One validator is the single gate: validateCharacter(obj) → { valid, errors, normalized }. Every construction path returns through it. An invalid character never enters state.

All creation paths converge on that schema:

rollCharacter(seed, constraints) — deterministic structured draw from weighted pools
expandCharacterProse(structured) — LLM writes flavor only: name, physical description, history paragraph, sample speech lines. It never selects traits and never invents fields.
authorCharacter(partial) — player supplies any subset of fields; everything unspecified is rolled. This is the default creation UX, not a special mode. Filling in three fields and rolling the rest must work as smoothly as filling in all of them.
editCharacter(id, patch) — explicit edit mode, validated the same way
importCharacter(json) — validated and version-migrated on entry

Any of these can be combined. A player may hand-write a personality, roll the history, and let the LLM write the prose.

Immutability, clarified: the bible is immutable to the LLM and to all runtime systems — nothing in SIM, NPC, or LLM may write to it. It is mutable only through explicit player edit mode, which increments a bibleRevision counter and appends a change record. Anti-drift is preserved; player authorship is not restricted.

Single characters export and import as standalone JSON, independent of a save. Players will want to share and reuse people they've made.

(d) Ambiguities / pushback
Cross-folder atomicity. kv.update is atomic per-key, but a move-out touches kv.npcs[id] (residency), kv.world:rooms (occupancy), and kv.world:castWeb (pair removal if last resident leaving). These can't be in one IDB transaction. I'll write in a defined order (NPC residency status first as source of truth, then cascade) and add a reconciliation pass on load that repairs any partial state from a crashed mid-operation. Acceptable, or do you want a different consistency model?

Character export scope. A character's pair relationships are bidirectional — exporting one character alone loses the other side. I propose: export = bible + bibleRevision + bibleChanges (the shareable identity). Import creates a new character with that bible and fresh mutable layers; pair relationships are rolled fresh for the new household. The bible is the thing worth sharing; relationships are game-specific. Confirm?

Rent contributor definition. Brief says "only residents contribute rent." A partner_of_resident is not a resident. So a partner moving in does not reduce per-resident rent (drama: "she's here every night but I'm still paying full share"). Only status: "resident" counts toward contributorCount. Confirm?

kv.images LRU. kv has no built-in LRU. I'll store { blob, lastAccess } per key and, on each cache write, if kv.images.keys().length > cap, scan for lowest lastAccess and delete until under cap. For a few hundred cached scenes this is trivial. The scan is async but non-blocking. Confirm approach?

kv.world key granularity. I'm splitting kv.world into separate keys (rooms, castWeb, quests, events, deliveries) so e.g. appending a cast-web pair only rewrites the castWeb object, not the entire world. Alternatively, one "world" key is simpler but rewrites everything on any change. I'll go with separate keys unless you object.

Tick granularity and event log growth. With up to 8 NPCs resolving every tick, I propose 30-min ticks (48/day) and pruning the event log to recent N entries (CONFIG-tunable) with older entries folded into NPC memory summaries during piggyback compaction. If you want finer/coarser ticks, say now — it affects schedule table resolution.

validateCharacter timing. I'll validate the structured draw (bible schema) after rollCharacter, validate prose additions (non-empty strings, length caps) after expandCharacterProse, then validate the complete bible as a unit before it enters state. An incomplete bible (e.g. prose failed but fell back to templates) must still pass validation — template fallbacks produce valid values. Confirm?

Guided mode vs author mode. The brief originally listed four generation modes (full random, guided, manual, seed entry) then later says authorCharacter(partial) is "the default creation UX." I'm treating author mode as the default: player fills any subset of fields, everything unspecified is rolled, prose is LLM-expanded. "Full random" = author mode with zero fields filled. "Guided" = author mode with constraint nudges. "Seed entry" = author mode with a fixed seed. This collapses four modes into one UX with options. Pushback if you wanted four distinct flows.

rollCharacter constraint satisfaction with 1 NPC. At the 1-resident tier, the brief says "reject flat characters harder" — require strong want, sharp wound, live blind spot. I'll implement this as a quality score threshold on the individual draw, rerolling if below threshold (capped attempts, then best-of with dropped constraints logged). But "reject harder" is subjective — I'll make the threshold a CONFIG number you can tune. OK?

Awaiting approval and answers to #1–9.

Scene participation cap (new constraint, applies across SIM/NPC/LLM/IMAGE):

No scene ever contains more than 3 conversational participants including the player — a hard maximum of 2 active NPCs. This is a system invariant, not a soft guideline.

Presence is tiered:

Active (max 2): full bible in context, generates dialogue, receives and applies relationship deltas, rendered as a character image layer.
Ambient (unlimited): physically present in the room, represented only by a one-line cached sketch and current activity. Narrated in scene description, visible in the right sidebar, but produces no dialogue and consumes no bible context. Rendered in the background layer or not at all.

Addressing an ambient NPC promotes them to active and demotes the least-engaged active NPC to ambient. Demotion must be narrated as an in-fiction beat — they turn back to their laptop, drift to the kitchen, get absorbed in their phone — never a silent swap.

Schedule resolution must actively avoid clustering. Apply a crowd-avoidance bias in SIM so NPCs distribute across rooms rather than piling into the living room. Rooms carry a soft comfort capacity in CONFIG; an NPC choosing a destination is weighted against rooms already at or above it. A full common room should be a deliberate, occasional event, not the default state.

Group set-pieces — house meetings, dinners, parties — are handled as narrated scenes: LLM receives compact sketches of everyone present and writes group narration describing the room, but individual dialogue still routes through a maximum of 2 active participants at a time. Never inject more than 2 bibles into a single call under any circumstance.

Because of this cap, bibles are only ever loaded for active participants. Every NPC's cached one-line sketch (generated once at character creation, stored on the bible) is what the ambient tier uses.

Rulings on his nine:

1. Cross-folder atomicity — approve, with one addition: write a pendingOp record to kv.meta before a multi-key operation and clear it after. Reconciliation on load reads that record and knows precisely what to repair instead of scanning for inconsistencies.

2. Character export — approve. Bible + revision + changes, relationships rolled fresh. Add a separate household export: all bibles plus the cast web, so a whole house can be shared, not just a person.

3. Rent — approve, and it's one of the better drama levers in the design. Make it data-driven rather than status-derived: put contributesRent: bool on residency, defaulting true for resident and false for partner_of_resident. Then whether the live-in girlfriend starts chipping in becomes a negotiable in-fiction outcome instead of a hardcoded rule.

4. Image LRU — approve the policy, reject the implementation. Don't scan kv.images.entries(); that pulls every Blob into memory to read a timestamp. Keep a lightweight imageIndex key in kv.meta mapping cache key → lastAccess, and evict from that. One small object read instead of hundreds of megabytes.

5. World key granularity — approve, separate keys.

6. Tick granularity — 30-minute ticks are fine. Add variable resolution: when the player sleeps or does a long work block, resolve the whole span in a single batched pass rather than sixteen individual ticks. Same tables, same determinism, a fraction of the work.

7. validateCharacter timing — approve as written.

8. Four modes collapsed into one — approve, it's the better design. Keep the four as UI entry points into the same form (buttons that preconfigure it) purely for discoverability.

9. Quality threshold — approve. One amendment: at 2+ residents the score must be relational, not just individual. A character who's compelling in isolation but generates no friction with the existing house should score low and reroll. What you're selecting for is the house, not the person.
